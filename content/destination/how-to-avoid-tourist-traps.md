# How to Avoid Tourist Traps: A Practical Guide

*By [Author] · [Date] · 7 min read*

A tourist trap is not a place with tourists in it. It is a business that has decided its
customers will never return, and prices, cooks and behaves accordingly.

That distinction matters, because the advice that follows from it is different from "avoid
crowds" — and some of the most crowded places in the world are entirely worth it.

---

## The Quick Answer

- **The defining feature is one-time customers.** Everything else follows from that.
- **Walk two streets** from any major sight before eating. Two streets is usually enough.
- **Photo menus, multiple languages, and touts outside** are the three reliable red flags.
- **Being crowded is not the same as being a trap.** The Sistine Chapel is crowded and
  extraordinary.
- **Ask a local a specific question**, not a general one.

---

## The Five Reliable Signals

**1. Someone is standing outside inviting you in.** A restaurant that needs to recruit from
the pavement is not filling on merit. This one signal alone is close to sufficient.

**2. The menu has photographs, or six languages.** Both mean the same thing: the customer is
assumed not to know the food and not to come back.

**3. It is directly adjacent to a major sight.** Rent on that spot is paid by the view, and
the food subsidises it. **Two streets away is a different economy.**

**4. It is open continuously all day.** Real kitchens in most food cultures close between
services. A place serving at 4pm in Italy or Spain is reheating.

**5. Nobody local is inside.** Look at who is eating. If everyone has a daypack and a camera,
you have your answer.

**The inverse signals, which are stronger:** a short handwritten menu, a queue of local
office workers at lunch, a single dish done well, and a room busy at the hour locals actually
eat — which is later than you think in Spain and Italy, earlier in Germany.

---

## Scams That Recur Worldwide

The same handful, with local variations.

- **The friendship bracelet / sprig of rosemary / free gift.** Something is placed in your
  hand or on your wrist, then payment is demanded. **Never accept anything handed to you.**
- **"The attraction is closed today."** Almost always untrue, and the person telling you has
  a shop or a tour to sell. **Verify at the entrance yourself.**
- **The broken taxi meter**, or the long route. Agree a price first or use a ride-hailing app.
- **Petition and clipboard distraction.** One person occupies you, another takes your bag.
- **The bill that grows.** Items you did not order, a cover charge, "bread service", or a
  price by weight for something quoted by portion. **Check the bill, every time.**
- **Currency confusion at exchange booths** — the "0% commission" booth making its money on a
  terrible rate. Use bank ATMs.
- **The spilled drink / mustard on your jacket.** Someone helps clean you up while your wallet
  goes.

**The common structure:** all of these begin with an unsolicited interaction initiated by a
stranger. That is the pattern to notice, rather than memorising the list.

## Finding the Good Version Instead

**Ask a specific question.** "Where's good to eat?" gets you the answer people think you want.
**"Where did you eat with your family last Sunday?"** gets you somewhere real. Ask your hotel's
cleaning or kitchen staff rather than the concierge, who may have arrangements.

**Use local-language search.** Searching the local-language phrase for the dish or "best" in
that city returns results written for locals rather than for visitors.

**Go where the transport goes at commuter hours.** Neighbourhoods a couple of metro stops out
from the centre are where people actually live and eat.

**Eat at local hours.** Lunch at 1pm in Madrid is early; dinner at 7pm in Rome is early. Being
in the room when locals are there is most of the battle.

**Look for the single-item specialist** — one dish, done for years. It appears in every food
culture and it is reliably the best version.

## When the Famous Thing Is Worth It

The opposite failure is real: a determination to avoid anything popular that produces a trip
missing the point.

**Crowded and genuinely worth it:** the Sistine Chapel, the Alhambra, Machu Picchu, Angkor
Wat, the Louvre, the Taj Mahal. These are crowded because they are extraordinary. The
strategy is timing — first entry, last entry, off-season — not avoidance.

**The distinction:** a *tourist trap* takes your money and gives you a poor version of
something. A *popular attraction* gives you the real thing, with other people also looking at
it. Confusing the two is how people end up skipping the Sistine Chapel and eating badly two
streets from it anyway.

---

## The Timing Rule

More useful than any list of places:

- **First entry of the day**, at almost any major sight, is a different experience.
- **The last two hours** before closing is the second-best window.
- **Lunchtime** is when coach groups move, so sites can quieten.
- **Shoulder season** solves more than any tactic.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How do you spot a tourist trap restaurant?**
Someone touting outside, photographs on the menu, a menu in several languages, a location
directly beside a major sight, continuous all-day service, and no local customers. Any two of
those together is enough to walk on.

**What is the most common travel scam?**
The unsolicited "gift" — a bracelet tied on your wrist, a sprig of herb pressed into your
hand — followed by a demand for payment. More broadly, almost every recurring scam begins with
an interaction a stranger initiates with you, which is the pattern worth noticing rather than
memorising individual variants.

**Are famous attractions worth visiting despite the crowds?**
Often yes. A tourist trap gives you a poor version of something and takes your money; a
crowded famous attraction gives you the real thing with other people present. For the latter,
the answer is timing — first entry, last entry or shoulder season — not avoidance.

**How do you find good local restaurants when travelling?**
Ask a specific question rather than a general one — "where did you eat with your family last
Sunday" beats "where's good" — search in the local language, walk two streets from any major
sight, and eat at the hours locals actually eat rather than when the restaurants near the
sights are empty and waiting.

---

## The Bottom Line

Walk two streets, never accept anything placed in your hand, ask specific questions rather
than general ones, and do not confuse a crowded masterpiece with a trap.
