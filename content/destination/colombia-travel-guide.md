# Colombia Travel Guide: Is It Safe? What to See? Where to Go?

*By [Author] · [Date] · 8 min read*

Colombia's reputation is roughly two decades out of date, and the correction has been
overdone in the other direction. The cities most travellers visit are far safer than the
1990s image suggests and less uniformly safe than the current enthusiasm implies.

The useful version is specific: which areas, which precautions, and which regions still carry
genuine government advisories.

---

## The Quick Answer

- **The main circuit** — Bogotá, Medellín, the coffee region, Cartagena — is well established
  and straightforward.
- **Petty crime and robbery are the real risks**, not the ones people imagine.
- **"No dar papaya"** is the local phrase and the whole philosophy: don't present an
  opportunity.
- **Some border and rural regions still carry serious government advisories.** These are
  specific and worth checking.
- **Best months:** December–March and July–August, though Colombia has no true off-season.

---

## Safety, Specifically

**What is genuinely common:** phone snatching, bag theft, robbery at knifepoint in the wrong
street at the wrong hour, and taxi-related scams. This is opportunistic urban crime, and it
is the thing to plan around.

**Practical measures, which locals take as standard:**

- **"No dar papaya"** — literally "don't give papaya", meaning don't present an easy
  opportunity. No visible phone on the street, no jewellery, no obviously expensive camera.
- **Use ride-hailing apps**, not street taxis.
- **Do not walk at night**, including in nice neighbourhoods.
- **Withdraw cash inside banks or malls**, not from street ATMs.
- **Ask your accommodation which streets are fine.** Advice is granular and locals give it
  freely.
- **Scopolamine** — a drug used to incapacitate and rob people, most often through a drink or
  offered food, and most often targeting people alone at night or on dating apps. This is
  well documented and specific: do not accept drinks or food from strangers, and do not go
  home with someone you have just met.

**Regional advisories:** parts of the country — particularly along some borders and in
specific rural departments — continue to carry serious government travel advisories relating
to armed groups. These are geographically specific and do not cover the main tourist circuit.
**Check your own government's current advisory** for the regions you plan to visit rather than
treating "Colombia" as a single answer.

## Where to Go

**Medellín** (3–4 days) — the transformation story, and genuinely pleasant year-round at
1,500 m. The metro and cable cars, Comuna 13 (go with a local guide from the neighbourhood),
El Poblado and Laureles. **Laureles** is where to stay if you want the city rather than the
expat scene.

**Bogotá** (2–3 days) — high (2,640 m — take the first day slowly), cold, and culturally the
richest city in the country. La Candelaria, the Gold Museum, Monserrate.

**The Coffee Region** (3 days) — Salento, the Cocora Valley with its wax palms, and coffee
farm stays. The most straightforwardly lovely part of Colombia.

**Cartagena** (2–3 days) — the walled Caribbean colonial city. Beautiful, hot, and the most
touristed place in the country. **Getsemaní** rather than the walled city for value and
atmosphere.

**Further:** **Tayrona** national park for Caribbean beaches, **Minca** for mountains and
birds, **San Gil** for adventure sports, **Guatapé** as a day trip from Medellín.

## Practicalities

- **Altitude:** Bogotá at 2,640 m affects most people for a day. Medellín at 1,500 m does not.
- **Domestic flights** are cheap and the country is large — the road from Bogotá to Medellín
  is long and winding.
- **Spanish helps enormously.** English is much less widely spoken than in Mexico or Costa
  Rica.
- **Cash and cards** both work; cards are widely accepted in cities.
- **Altitude and coffee:** the good coffee is largely exported, but the specialty scene in
  Medellín and Bogotá is now excellent.

## When to Go

Colombia is near the equator, so it has wet and dry seasons rather than summer and winter, and
these vary by region.

| Period | Conditions |
|---|---|
| **Dec–Mar** | Driest across most of the country. **Peak** |
| Apr–May | Wetter |
| **Jul–Aug** | Second dry window |
| Sept–Nov | Wettest in much of the country |

**Medellín is "the city of eternal spring"** and sits at roughly the same temperature all
year, which makes it a reliable base whenever you go.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is Colombia safe for tourists?**
The main circuit — Bogotá, Medellín, the coffee region and Cartagena — is well established and
most visitors have no trouble. The real risks are opportunistic: phone snatching, robbery, and
drink spiking with scopolamine, particularly targeting people alone at night. Some border and
rural regions continue to carry serious government advisories; check your own government's
current advice for specific regions rather than for the country as a whole.

**What does "no dar papaya" mean?**
Literally "don't give papaya" — the Colombian phrase for not presenting an easy opportunity.
In practice it means no visible phone on the street, no jewellery, no obvious camera, and not
putting yourself somewhere isolated at night.

**How long do you need in Colombia?**
Two weeks covers Bogotá, Medellín, the coffee region and Cartagena comfortably. Three weeks
lets you add Tayrona, Minca or San Gil without rushing.

**Where should you stay in Medellín?**
Laureles for the city as residents experience it, or El Poblado for the more international
scene with more hotels and nightlife. Laureles is better value and more interesting; El
Poblado is easier if it is your first time.

---

## The Bottom Line

Stick to the main circuit, practise "no dar papaya", use apps rather than street taxis, and
check regional advisories rather than treating Colombia as one answer.
