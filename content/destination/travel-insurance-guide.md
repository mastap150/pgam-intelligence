# How to Choose Travel Insurance: What Actually Matters (And What Doesn't)

*By [Author] · [Date] · 9 min read*

Most travel insurance comparison happens on the wrong number. People sort by price, check
that "medical" appears in the list, and buy the cheapest. Then a scooter accident in Thailand
turns into a five-figure bill that the policy declines, because they did not hold the right
licence.

There are four things worth checking on a travel insurance policy. Price is not one of them.

---

## The Quick Answer

- **Medical evacuation limit** is the number that matters most. Aim for **$250,000+**.
- **Check the activity exclusions.** Scooters, diving, trekking above altitude and skiing
  off-piste are commonly excluded or need an add-on.
- **Pre-existing conditions** must usually be declared, and often need buying within a window
  of your first trip payment.
- **Your credit card may already cover some of this** — check before buying twice.
- **Cancellation cover only pays for listed reasons.** "I changed my mind" is not one.

---

## What You Are Actually Insuring Against

Ranked by financial severity, not by how often people claim.

### 1. Medical evacuation — the catastrophic one

An air ambulance from a remote location to a hospital that can treat you, and if necessary
onward to your home country, runs into six figures. From a Himalayan trek, a Pacific island
or a safari camp, it is the single largest financial risk of the trip.

**This is the number to sort policies by.** $250,000 is a sensible floor; $500,000 to
$1,000,000 is better and rarely costs much more.

### 2. Emergency medical treatment

Hospital care abroad. In the United States this is ruinous; in much of the world it is
manageable but still substantial. Look for **direct billing** — a policy that pays the
hospital rather than reimbursing you months later matters enormously when you are the one
being admitted.

### 3. Trip cancellation and interruption

Reimburses non-refundable costs when you cannot travel. **Only for listed reasons** —
typically illness, injury, death in the family, jury duty, certain natural disasters. It
covers a percentage of insured trip cost, so it only makes sense if you have significant
prepaid, non-refundable bookings.

### 4. Baggage and delay

The one everyone thinks about and the least financially serious. Limits are low, per-item
caps are lower, and electronics are often excluded or heavily capped.

---

## The Four Checks

### Check 1: Medical evacuation and repatriation limit

As above. If a policy does not state one clearly, that is informative.

### Check 2: The activity list

This is where most declined claims originate.

**Commonly excluded or requiring an add-on:**

- **Riding a motorbike or scooter** — and where covered, usually only if you hold a valid
  licence for that category *and* wear a helmet. This voids more claims than anything else
  on this list.
- **Scuba diving** — often limited by depth, and requiring certification.
- **Trekking above a stated altitude** — commonly 3,000–4,500 m. Relevant for Nepal, Peru,
  Kilimanjaro.
- **Skiing and snowboarding**, particularly off-piste.
- **Climbing, canyoning, bungee, skydiving.**

**What to do:** write down every activity you might plausibly do, then read the policy
wording against that list. Not the marketing page — the wording document.

### Check 3: Pre-existing conditions

Most policies exclude pre-existing conditions unless declared, and many require the policy
be bought **within a set window of your first trip payment** — often 14 to 21 days — to
qualify for a waiver.

**If you have any ongoing condition, buy early.** Waiting until the week before you fly can
disqualify you from cover you would otherwise have had.

### Check 4: What you already have

Do not buy cover you hold twice.

- **Premium credit cards** frequently include trip cancellation, delay and sometimes medical
  cover — usually conditional on paying for the trip with that card.
- **Some health insurance** extends abroad; most does not, and almost none covers evacuation.
- **EHIC/GHIC** covers state healthcare within Europe for eligible residents. It is not
  insurance and does not cover repatriation.

## Types of Policy

| Type | Best for | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **Single trip** | One holiday with fixed dates | Cheapest per trip; buy early for cancellation cover |
| **Annual multi-trip** | 3+ trips a year | Per-trip day limits, often 30–45 days |
| **Long-stay / nomad** | Months abroad, no fixed return | Often medical-only; thin cancellation cover |
| **Adventure add-on** | Trekking, diving, skiing | Verify the specific activity and altitude |
| **Specialist medical** | Pre-existing conditions | Costs more; declines less |

---

## When It Genuinely Matters Most

Insurance is not equally valuable on every trip. It matters most when:

- **You are far from good hospitals.** Remote trekking, safari, small islands, expedition
  cruises.
- **The trip cost is large and non-refundable.** Safari, expedition cruise, a booked-out
  hut-to-hut trek.
- **You are doing something with real injury risk.** Riding, diving, climbing, skiing.
- **You are travelling to the United States**, where medical costs are in a category of
  their own.

It matters least on a short city break in Western Europe with refundable bookings and a
credit card that already covers delay.

## What It Costs

Roughly, as a share of trip cost or a flat rate.

| Policy | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Single trip, Europe, 1 week | $25–$60 |
| Single trip, worldwide incl. US, 2 weeks | $60–$150 |
| Annual multi-trip, worldwide | $150–$400 |
| Long-stay / nomad, monthly | $45–$100 per month |
| Comprehensive with cancellation | Typically **4–8% of insured trip cost** |

**The rule of thumb:** medical-only cover is cheap. Cancellation cover is what makes policies
expensive, because it scales with your trip cost. If your bookings are refundable, you may
only need the medical half.

## How to Actually Claim

The claim is decided by what you did at the time, not by what you say afterwards.

1. **Contact the insurer's emergency line before treatment** where it is not an emergency.
   Many policies require pre-authorisation and decline without it.
2. **Keep everything.** Receipts, medical reports, police reports for theft — usually
   required within 24 hours of the incident.
3. **Get the diagnosis in writing**, ideally in English.
4. **Photograph damaged luggage** before anyone takes it away.
5. **Claim quickly.** Deadlines are commonly 30–90 days from the incident.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I really need travel insurance?**
It depends on the trip. For a short European city break with refundable bookings and a credit
card that covers delays, it is optional. For anywhere remote, anywhere with high medical
costs, any trip with large non-refundable prepayments, or any activity with real injury risk,
the medical evacuation cover alone justifies it — an air ambulance runs into six figures.

**What is the most important thing in a travel insurance policy?**
The medical evacuation and repatriation limit. Aim for at least $250,000. It is the only
element that covers a genuinely catastrophic cost, and it is the number most comparison sites
bury.

**Does travel insurance cover riding a scooter?**
Often not, and this is the single most common cause of declined claims. Where it is covered,
policies typically require that you hold a valid licence for that vehicle category and wear a
helmet. Check the policy wording before renting, not after an accident.

**When should I buy travel insurance?**
As soon as you make your first non-refundable booking. Cancellation cover only applies to
what you buy after the policy starts, and many pre-existing-condition waivers require the
policy be purchased within roughly 14–21 days of your first trip payment.

---

## The Bottom Line

Sort policies by medical evacuation limit, read the activity exclusions against what you
actually intend to do, and buy it the day you make your first non-refundable booking.
