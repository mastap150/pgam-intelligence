# Mexico City Travel Guide: Neighbourhoods, Food, Safety & Getting Around

*By [Author] · [Date] · 9 min read*

Mexico City is one of the great food cities and one of the most misunderstood destinations
in the Americas. It is also at **2,240 metres**, which is high enough that your first day
will not go the way you planned if you ignore it.

This guide covers where to stay, what to eat, how to move around, and an honest read on
safety — a subject on which most coverage is either alarmist or evasive.

---

## The Quick Answer

- **Stay in Roma Norte, Condesa or Juárez** for a first visit. Coyoacán if you want quieter.
- **The altitude is real.** Take the first day slowly and go easy on alcohol.
- **The Metro is excellent and cheap**; Uber is cheap enough to default to at night.
- **Safety is neighbourhood-specific**, not city-wide. The tourist areas are fine with normal
  precautions.
- **Best months:** March–May and October–November.

---

## Altitude, First

At 2,240 m, Mexico City is higher than Kathmandu. Most people feel something for 24–48
hours: breathlessness on stairs, a mild headache, poor sleep, and alcohol hitting notably
harder.

**What to do:** take the first day slowly, drink more water than usual, go light on alcohol
the first night, and put anything strenuous — Teotihuacán, cycling, the volcanoes — on day
three rather than day one.

## Where to Stay

| Colonia | Best for | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Roma Norte** | First-time visitors | Tree-lined, walkable, dense with restaurants and cafés. The default for a reason |
| **Condesa** | Green space, calm | Art deco, parks, quieter than Roma at night |
| **Juárez** | Central position | Between Roma and the historic centre; improving fast |
| **Polanco** | Upscale, museums | Expensive, more corporate, excellent restaurants |
| **Coyoacán** | Atmosphere, quiet | Colonial, cobbled, Frida Kahlo's house. Further out |
| **Centro Histórico** | Sights on the doorstep | Extraordinary architecture, busy by day, quiet and less appealing at night |

**For a first visit: Roma Norte.** Walkable, safe, well connected, and you can eat
exceptionally without leaving the neighbourhood.

**Note on Roma and Condesa:** these are the neighbourhoods most affected by the remote-work
influx, and rents and prices have risen sharply, which has become a genuine local grievance.
Worth being aware of rather than oblivious to.

## What to Do

### The essentials

- **Museo Nacional de Antropología** — one of the world's great museums, and the single best
  thing in the city. Give it half a day minimum.
- **Centro Histórico** — the Zócalo, the Metropolitan Cathedral, the Templo Mayor excavation,
  and the Palacio de Bellas Artes.
- **Teotihuacán** — the pyramids, an hour out. Go early; note that climbing is restricted at
  several structures.
- **Coyoacán and the Casa Azul** — Frida Kahlo's house. **Book tickets well ahead**, it sells
  out.
- **Xochimilco** — the canals and trajineras. Best with a group; go on a Saturday for the
  atmosphere or a weekday for calm.

### Worth the detour

- **Museo Jumex** and **Museo Soumaya** in Polanco, next door to each other.
- **Luis Barragán's house** — the architect's home, by advance booking only. Extraordinary.
- **Mercado de la Merced** or **Mercado de San Juan** for the scale of the food markets.
- **Lucha libre** at Arena México. Genuinely a great night out.

## Eating

This is the reason to come.

**Street and market:**

- **Tacos al pastor** — pork on a vertical spit, carved with pineapple. The city's signature.
- **Tacos de canasta, suadero, carnitas** — each stand specialises; that is the point.
- **Tlacoyos** — blue corn, stuffed, from a comal on the street.
- **Quesadillas** — note that in Mexico City these do not automatically contain cheese. You
  must ask for it. This confuses everyone once.
- **Esquites and elote** — street corn, in a cup or on the cob.
- **Tortas** and **tamales** for breakfast.

**Sit-down:** the city has one of the strongest fine-dining scenes in the Americas, and
mid-range restaurants in Roma and Condesa are excellent value by North American or European
standards.

**Practical:** eat where the queue is local and turnover is high, same as anywhere. Tap water
is not drinkable; restaurants use purified water and ice, which is generally fine.

## Getting Around

- **Metro** — extremely cheap, extensive, and fast. Crowded at rush hour; there are
  women-only carriages at the front on many lines.
- **Metrobús** — dedicated-lane buses, easy to use, same fare card as the Metro.
- **Uber and Didi** — cheap, widely used, and the sensible default at night. Preferable to
  hailing a street taxi.
- **Walking** — Roma, Condesa, Juárez and Coyoacán are all genuinely walkable. The city as a
  whole is not.
- **Ecobici** bike share — good in the central colonias.

## Safety: an Honest Read

Mexico City's reputation is shaped by news about other parts of the country. The cartel
violence that dominates coverage is largely not a Mexico City phenomenon, and it is not
something visitors to the central colonias encounter.

**What is actually a risk:**

- **Petty theft and pickpocketing** — on the Metro at rush hour, in crowded markets, in the
  Centro. The main practical concern.
- **Express kidnapping** — rare but real, and the reason to use Uber or Didi rather than
  hailing a street taxi, particularly at night.
- **Card skimming** — use ATMs inside banks rather than on the street.
- **Neighbourhood variation is sharp.** Areas a few kilometres from the tourist colonias are
  genuinely not for casual wandering. Ask locally rather than assuming.

**Sensible practice:** normal big-city awareness, app-based transport at night, don't display
a phone on the Metro, and don't walk into unfamiliar neighbourhoods after dark.

**Earthquakes:** the city sits on a former lakebed and is seismically active. Learn what the
alarm sounds like and where your accommodation's assembly point is. This is routine local
knowledge, not alarmism.

## When to Go

| Period | Conditions | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Mar–May** | Warm, dry, before the rains | **Best** |
| Jun–Sept | Rainy season — heavy afternoon storms, mornings usually clear | Workable, green, cheaper |
| **Oct–Nov** | Dry, mild. **Día de Muertos (1–2 Nov)** | **Best**, book far ahead for Muertos |
| Dec–Feb | Dry, cool, occasionally cold at night | Good; clear air |

**Día de Muertos** is extraordinary and the city is booked out months ahead. Decide early.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is Mexico City safe for tourists?**
The central colonias — Roma, Condesa, Juárez, Polanco, Coyoacán — are safe with normal
big-city precautions. The cartel violence that dominates international coverage of Mexico is
largely not a Mexico City phenomenon. The real risks are petty theft on the Metro and in
crowded areas, and the reason to use Uber or Didi rather than street taxis at night.

**Where should I stay in Mexico City?**
Roma Norte for a first visit — walkable, well connected and dense with good food. Condesa is
quieter and greener, Juárez is more central, Coyoacán has the most atmosphere but is further
out.

**Does the altitude in Mexico City affect you?**
Often, for the first day or two. At 2,240 m most visitors notice breathlessness on stairs,
disrupted sleep and alcohol having a stronger effect. Take the first day slowly, drink extra
water, and schedule anything strenuous for day three.

**How many days do you need in Mexico City?**
Four to five for the city itself plus Teotihuacán. A week lets you add Xochimilco, more
museums and a day trip to Puebla without rushing.

---

## The Bottom Line

Stay in Roma Norte, treat the first day as an altitude day, use Uber after dark, and plan the
trip around the food rather than the sights.
