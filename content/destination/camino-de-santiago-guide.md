# Walking the Camino de Santiago: What Nobody Tells You Before You Go

*By [Author] · [Date] · 10 min read*

Roughly 500,000 people collected a Compostela in Santiago in 2025, and 2027 will be busier
— it is a Xacobeo Holy Year, which happens when 25 July, the Feast of Saint James, falls on
a Sunday. The Holy Door of the cathedral stays open all year and pilgrim numbers reach
levels that break records.

If you have been thinking about walking the Camino de Santiago, 2027 is either the best
year to do it or the worst, depending entirely on what you want from it. This guide covers
which route to choose, the Compostela rules that quietly changed in 2025, what it actually
costs, and how the Holy Year should change your planning.

---

## The Quick Answer

- **You do not need to walk 780 km.** 100 km on foot qualifies for the Compostela.
- **The rules changed in 2025.** Any 100 km on an official route now counts, provided your
  final stage ends in Santiago. You also need **two stamps per day**, for the whole walk.
- **2027 is a Holy Year.** Next ones after that: 2032, 2038, 2049.
- **Budget €35–€55/day** walking independently, sleeping in albergues.
- **Book nothing in advance** on the classic route — except in 2027, when you should.

---

## Why 2027 Is Different

Holy Years follow a 6–5–6–11 year cycle, so they are not rare enough to be once-in-a-lifetime
but not common enough to ignore. During a Xacobeo the Holy Door on the cathedral's east
face is opened for the calendar year, a plenary indulgence is available to Catholic
pilgrims, and there are ceremonies and events that happen in no other year.

The practical consequence is crowding. The last full Holy Year drew a surge, and the Camino
has grown every year since — half a million Compostelas in 2025, from a base of around
270,000 a decade earlier.

**What that means for you, concretely:**

- **The last 100 km will be intense.** Sarria to Santiago in July and August 2027 will be a
  procession. If your idea of a pilgrimage involves solitude, this is not where you find it.
- **Beds will not be reliably available on arrival.** The traditional Camino rhythm — walk
  until you are tired, take whatever albergue has space — assumes slack in the system. A
  Holy Year removes it on the popular stretches.
- **The alternative routes become the smart choice.** The Norte, the Primitivo and the
  Inglés absorb a fraction of the traffic.

**If you want the Holy Year:** go, and accept the crowds as part of it. There is genuine
atmosphere in a cathedral city full of people who all walked there.

**If you want the walk:** consider 2026, or walk in 2027 but pick a quiet route and go in
May or late September.

## The Compostela Rules Changed in 2025 — Most Guides Are Out of Date

This trips people up because the majority of Camino content online was written before the
change.

**What's required now:**

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| Distance | 100 km on foot, or 200 km by bicycle |
| Which 100 km | **Any** 100 km on an official Camino route — no longer required to be the final 100 km — provided your last stage finishes in Santiago |
| Credencial stamps | **Two per day, for your entire walk** — not just the final stretch, as previously |
| Where to collect | The Pilgrim's Office in Santiago, presenting your completed credencial |
| What happens there | You are asked briefly about your route and your motivation, then issued the certificate |

The two-stamps-a-day rule is the one that catches people. Stamps come from albergues, cafés,
churches, town halls and pilgrim-friendly shops, and they are easy to collect — but only if
you know you need them from day one. Turning up with a sparse credencial is a
disappointing way to end a long walk.

The distance change is more generous than it sounds: it opens up the option of walking a
beautiful section somewhere else on the route and then completing the final approach into
Santiago, rather than being locked into the busiest stretch.

## Which Route Should You Walk?

There is no single Camino. There are dozens of waymarked routes converging on one city.
These are the ones worth considering.

### Camino Francés — 772 km, 30–35 days — the default

Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port over the Pyrenees, across the meseta, into Galicia. It is the route
in the films and books, it has the densest albergue network and the strongest social
scene, and it is the busiest by a wide margin. If you want to meet people and never worry
about logistics, this is the one.

**The honest caveat:** the meseta — the flat central plateau — is 200 km of wheat and sky
that some pilgrims describe as the most profound part of the walk and others find close to
unbearable. Know which of those you are likely to be.

### Camino Portugués — 240–280 km from Porto, 10–14 days — the best first Camino

Porto north through the Minho, across into Galicia at Tui. Shorter, flatter, warmer, and it
has a coastal variant that trades some of the historic route for the Atlantic. Two weeks is
achievable for most people with a normal holiday allowance, which the Francés is not.

For most first-timers with limited time, this is the route I would point them at.

### Camino del Norte — 825 km, 35–40 days — for the scenery

The Basque and Cantabrian coast, through San Sebastián, Bilbao and Santander. Much harder
than the Francés — constant climbing in and out of coastal valleys — and considerably
quieter. Better food than any other route, by some distance.

### Camino Primitivo — 321 km, 14–16 days — the hardest

The original route, from Oviedo through the Asturian mountains. Steep, remote, sparse in
services, and the one experienced pilgrims most often name as their favourite. Not a first
Camino.

### Camino Inglés — 120 km from Ferrol, ~6 days — the short one that qualifies

The route medieval pilgrims arriving by sea from Britain and Ireland walked. Just over the
100 km threshold from Ferrol, so it earns a Compostela in under a week.

**The gotcha:** starting from A Coruña instead of Ferrol leaves you short of 100 km. People
get this wrong and are disappointed at the Pilgrim's Office. Start at Ferrol.

### The 100 km options

If you have a week: **Sarria to Santiago** (~115 km, Francés — busiest by far),
**Tui to Santiago** (~115 km, Portugués — much calmer), or the **Inglés from Ferrol**.

In a Holy Year, take Tui or Ferrol.

## What It Actually Costs

Per person per day, walking independently.

| Style | Daily cost | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Albergue / bare bones | €25–€35 | Municipal albergues (€8–€12), supermarket food, pilgrim menus |
| Standard independent | €35–€55 | Mix of municipal and private albergues, a pilgrim menu most nights, coffee and beer |
| Private rooms | €70–€110 | Pensións and small hotels, booked a day or two ahead |
| Guided group tour | €3,000–€5,000 for the week | Hotels, luggage transfer, guide, meals, transfers |

Add flights, plus €40–€80 for boots you have properly broken in and a credencial (€2–€3
from a pilgrim association or your first albergue).

**The single biggest cost variable is luggage transfer.** Services like the Correos
mochila scheme move your pack between stages for roughly €5–€8 a leg. Purists object.
Anyone with a knee that has opinions will consider it money well spent.

## When to Go

| Period | Conditions | Crowds |
|---|---|---|
| April–May | Cool, green, some rain. Excellent walking. | Moderate — rising fast |
| June | Warm, long days, reliable | Heavy |
| July–August | Hot on the meseta (35 °C+), busiest | Very heavy — extreme in 2027 |
| September | Warm, harvest, arguably the best month | Heavy but easing |
| October | Cooler, quieter, beautiful in Galicia | Light |
| November–March | Wet and cold; many albergues shut; Pyrenees route may be impassable | Very light |

**Best overall: late September into early October.** Second choice: May.

Avoid mid-July to late August in 2027 unless the Holy Year atmosphere is specifically what
you are going for.

## Should You Book Ahead, or Walk It the Old Way?

The traditional answer is no — you walk, you stop when you stop, and a bed appears. That
answer is getting less reliable every year, and in a Holy Year it stops being sound advice
on the popular routes.

**My recommendation for 2027:**

- **Book your first two nights and your last two nights.** Arrival and Santiago itself are
  the two points where being stuck is genuinely disruptive.
- **On the Francés in summer, book a day ahead as you go.** You lose a little spontaneity;
  you gain not walking an extra 8 km at 6pm to find a bed.
- **On the Norte, Primitivo or Inglés, keep walking the old way.** The pressure is not there.
- **Municipal albergues generally cannot be booked** — they are first-come, and in high
  season that means arriving by early afternoon.

## Walking It Alone — and the Group Option

The Camino is the most solo-friendly long walk in Europe. It is waymarked to the point
where getting lost takes effort, there are other walkers within sight most of the day, and
the albergue system creates a nightly social structure without requiring anyone to organise
anything. Solo travellers, and solo women in particular, consistently report it as one of
the more comfortable places to walk alone in Europe.

That said, "solo" on the Camino rarely means alone. You fall in with people walking your
pace and lose them and find them again. That is most of what people mean when they say the
Camino changed something for them.

**Where a guided group genuinely helps:** if you have a hard week-long window and no
tolerance for logistics failing, if you want your bag moved and a bed guaranteed every
night, or if walking into a foreign country alone is the thing stopping you from going at
all. Operators running small-group Camino trips — EF Adventures among them, including
departures built specifically for solo travellers — handle accommodation, transfers and
luggage, typically over a week on the final Galician stages.

The trade-off is real and worth naming: you get certainty and lose the improvisation that
many pilgrims say was the point. You are also paying roughly ten times the independent
daily cost.

## What Nobody Tells You

- **Your feet are the whole trip.** Blisters end more Caminos than fitness does. Break the
  boots in over at least 100 km at home. Carry proper tape, not plasters.
- **Train with the pack, not without it.** Walking 20 km is a different activity from
  walking 20 km carrying 8 kg.
- **8 kg is the target.** Every experienced pilgrim you meet will have posted something
  home from Burgos.
- **The second week is when people quit** — not the first. The novelty has gone and the end
  is not yet close.
- **Bedbugs exist.** Not everywhere, not often, but check mattress seams and keep your bag
  off the floor.
- **The Pilgrim's Office queue can run to hours in high season.** In 2027, expect worse.
- **Nobody cares why you are walking.** Religious, athletic, grieving, curious, or between
  jobs — the question "why are you here" gets asked constantly and every answer is accepted.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long does the Camino de Santiago take?**
It depends entirely on the route. The full Camino Francés is 772 km and takes 30–35 days.
The Camino Portugués from Porto is 240–280 km over 10–14 days. The minimum for a
Compostela — 100 km on foot — takes most people 5 to 7 days.

**Do I need to be religious to walk the Camino?**
No. The Pilgrim's Office asks about your motivation when issuing the Compostela, and
non-religious answers are entirely normal — a large share of modern pilgrims walk for
cultural, athletic or personal reasons. A separate certificate of distance is available
alongside the Compostela.

**Is the Camino de Santiago safe to walk alone?**
It is among the most solo-friendly long-distance walks in Europe: heavily waymarked, busy
enough that you are rarely out of sight of other walkers, and supported by a dense albergue
network. Standard precautions apply, and the usual advice about walking sections in the
dark holds here as anywhere.

**How much does the Camino de Santiago cost?**
Budget €35–€55 per person per day walking independently and sleeping in albergues, or
€25–€35 at the bare-bones end. A guided week-long group trip runs €3,000–€5,000 including
hotels, meals, guiding and luggage transfer.

## The Bottom Line

2027's Holy Year makes the Camino de Santiago more atmospheric and considerably more
crowded, so choose your route accordingly — Francés if you want the full social experience,
Portugués or Inglés if you want to walk without queueing. Whichever you pick, collect two
stamps a day from the first morning.
