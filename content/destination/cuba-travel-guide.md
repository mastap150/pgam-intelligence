# Cuba Travel Guide: What Americans Need to Know Before Visiting

> **This article describes rules, not legal advice.** US regulations on Cuba change with
> administrations and are enforced by the Treasury Department. Verify the current position
> with official US government sources before booking anything.

*By [Author] · [Date] · 8 min read*

Cuba is straightforward for most of the world and complicated for Americans, and almost all
the confusion comes from one distinction: **the US embargo prohibits tourist travel to Cuba
for anyone subject to US jurisdiction** — citizens, permanent residents, and anyone physically
present in the United States regardless of nationality — **while twelve authorised categories
of travel remain permitted.**

That is the whole legal picture. Everything else is practical.

---

## The Quick Answer

- **US persons cannot travel to Cuba as tourists.** Twelve authorised categories exist.
- **Everyone else** — Canadians, Europeans, most of the world — can visit as tourists
  normally.
- **Cuba now requires an electronic visa** (e-Visa), which replaced the old paper tourist
  cards, plus the online **D'Viajeros** entry form.
- **Proof of medical insurance valid in Cuba is required**; many airlines include it in the
  fare.
- **Bring cash.** US-issued cards do not work, and the money situation is genuinely
  complicated.

---

## The US Rules

**Tourist travel is prohibited.** Twelve categories of authorised travel remain, covering
things like family visits, journalism, professional research, educational activities,
religious activities, and humanitarian projects.

**"Support for the Cuban People"** has historically been the category most independent
travellers use. It requires a full-time schedule of activities supporting Cuban civil society
— staying in privately-owned *casas particulares* rather than state hotels, eating in private
*paladares*, hiring independent guides — and **keeping records of that schedule for five
years.**

**What that means practically:**

- **You self-certify** the category; you do not apply for permission in advance.
- **Keep a written itinerary and receipts.** The record-keeping requirement is real.
- **Some accommodation and businesses are on a restricted list** and dealing with them is
  prohibited. Check the current list before booking.
- **The rules change.** They have tightened and loosened repeatedly across administrations.

**Verify the current position** with the US Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control and
the State Department before booking. Do not rely on this or any other travel article for the
legal position.

---

## Entry Requirements for Everyone

- **E-Visa** — Cuba moved to a mandatory electronic visa, replacing the old paper tourist
  card, for all travellers including US citizens.
- **D'Viajeros form** — an online customs and health declaration completed before arrival, by
  all travellers.
- **Medical insurance valid in Cuba** — required, and frequently bundled into the airfare by
  airlines flying the route. Carry proof.
- **Passport** valid for the duration of stay.

## Money — the Part That Catches People Out

This is where practical trips go wrong.

- **US-issued cards do not work in Cuba.** No cards, no ATMs. This includes cards from
  US banks issued to non-Americans.
- **Bring enough cash for the entire trip**, plus a margin. There is no backup.
- **Euros, pounds and Canadian dollars** generally exchange better than US dollars, which
  have at times attracted an additional penalty.
- **The exchange situation is complicated and shifts** — there have been multiple official
  and unofficial rates in recent years. Research the current position immediately before you
  travel, not months ahead.
- **Non-US cards** (European, Canadian) work in some places but unreliably. Do not depend on
  them.

## Where to Stay and Eat

**Casas particulares** — privately-owned guest rooms, usually in family homes. These are the
characteristic Cuban stay, they are far better value than state hotels, and for US travellers
using the Support for the Cuban People category they are effectively required. $25–$60 a
night, often including a substantial breakfast.

**Paladares** — privately-run restaurants, consistently better than state ones.

**Note on supply:** Cuba has faced significant shortages of food, fuel and medicine, and
power cuts are common. This affects visitors — restaurants run out of menu items, and
blackouts are routine. Casas often have generators. Go with realistic expectations and a
degree of flexibility.

---

## Where to Go

**Havana** (3–4 days) — Habana Vieja, the Malecón, Vedado. The city is the reason most people
come.

**Viñales** (2 days) — tobacco country, limestone *mogotes*, horse riding. Three hours west
and the best countryside in Cuba.

**Trinidad** (2 days) — a preserved colonial town, cobbled and colourful, with beaches nearby.

**Cienfuegos, Santa Clara** — worthwhile stops on the road east.

**Santiago de Cuba** — the far east, culturally distinct, and a long way. For a longer trip.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can Americans travel to Cuba?**
Not as tourists. The US embargo prohibits tourist travel for anyone subject to US jurisdiction
— citizens, permanent residents and anyone physically present in the US. Twelve authorised
categories of travel remain permitted, including the "Support for the Cuban People" category
most independent travellers use, which requires a full-time qualifying schedule and
record-keeping for five years. Verify the current rules with official US government sources.

**Do you need a visa for Cuba?**
Yes. Cuba now requires a mandatory electronic visa, which replaced the old paper tourist
cards, for all travellers including US citizens. All arrivals must also complete the online
D'Viajeros entry form and hold medical insurance valid in Cuba.

**Can you use credit cards in Cuba?**
US-issued cards do not work at all, including US-bank cards held by non-Americans. Bring
enough cash for the entire trip plus a margin. Non-US cards work in some places but
unreliably.

**Where should you stay in Cuba?**
Casas particulares — privately-owned rooms in family homes, at $25–$60 a night. They are
better value than state hotels, more interesting, and for US travellers using the Support for
the Cuban People category they are effectively part of qualifying.

---

## The Bottom Line

If you are not a US person, Cuba is a straightforward trip that needs an e-visa and a lot of
cash. If you are, the category you travel under is a legal question to settle with official
sources before you book anything.
