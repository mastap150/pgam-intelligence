# Hiking in the Dolomites: The Guide That Starts With a Calendar

*By [Author] · [Date] · 9 min read*

Most Dolomites guides open with the scenery. This one opens with a date, because the date is
what determines whether your trip happens at all: **the mountain huts open their booking
systems between December and February, and by March the peak July and August weeks are
entirely sold out.**

Everything else — which route, how hard, what it costs — is a decision you can make later.
The booking window is not. If you are reading this in autumn, you are early, which is
exactly where you want to be.

---

## The Quick Answer

- **Season:** mid-June to end of September. Best conditions mid-July to mid-September.
- **Booking:** rifugios open reservations December–February for the following summer. Peak
  weeks gone by March.
- **The classic route:** Alta Via 1 — 125 km, Lago di Braies to Belluno, 8–12 days.
- **You do not need a multi-day trek.** The Dolomites are unusually good for day hiking
  from a fixed base.
- **Budget €70–€95 per night** in a rifugio with half board.

---

## Why the Dolomites Are Different

These are not the Alps in a different country. The Dolomites are limestone, and limestone
does something granite does not: it stands in vertical walls and detached towers rather than
sloping to a summit. The result is that you walk through the mountains rather than up them —
a trail contours along a wall, rounds a corner, and the entire landscape changes.

The second difference is the hut system. **Rifugios** are not survival shelters. They are
staffed mountain inns serving three courses and wine at 2,500 m, with beds, hot showers and
in some cases a road-accessible terrace full of day visitors. You can walk for two weeks
across high mountains carrying a daypack, sleeping indoors and eating properly every night.

That combination — dramatic terrain, no camping gear, no cooking — is why the Dolomites
convert more people into mountain walkers than anywhere else in Europe. It is also why
everything books out.

## The Booking Calendar

This is the section to act on.

| When | What happens |
|---|---|
| **December–February** | Rifugios open reservations for the coming summer. Systems vary — some open a fixed date, some 12 months out, some only 3 months before the season |
| **By March** | July and August fully booked at the popular huts on the popular routes |
| **April & November** | Many huts closed for maintenance |
| **Mid-June** | Season opens; snow may still be on high passes |
| **Mid-July to mid-September** | Prime conditions — trails clear, everything open |
| **End of September** | Huts close for the season |

**What to do about it:**

1. **Fix your dates before December.** You cannot book without them, and the window is
   short.
2. **Book the huts before the flights.** This is backwards from every other trip and it is
   correct here. Flights to Venice or Innsbruck are always available. A bed at Rifugio
   Lagazuoi on 8 August is not.
3. **Book the whole chain in one sitting.** A hut-to-hut route only works if every night
   connects. One missing link means a very long day or a bail-out.
4. **Private rooms need the longest lead** — six months or more. Dormitory beds hold on
   longer.
5. **Check each hut individually.** There is no single booking system; the Club Alpino
   Italiano listing is the most complete directory of huts and contact details.

**If you have missed the window:** go in mid-June or the second half of September, or build
a day-hiking trip from a valley base instead. Both are genuinely good options rather than
consolation prizes.

## Choosing Your Route

### Alta Via 1 — 125 km, 8–12 days — the classic

Lago di Braies to Belluno, north to south through the eastern Dolomites. Nine days is the
standard pace. It is the most popular high route in the range and the one to pick if you
want the full traverse: no technical climbing, no via ferrata required, no scrambling beyond
the occasional cabled section.

**Fitness needed:** you should be comfortable with 5–7 hours of walking and 800–1,000 m of
ascent, repeatedly, for over a week. It is a demanding walk rather than a technical one.

**The catch:** it finishes at Belluno, which is not where you started, and the logistics of
getting back are worth planning before you go.

### Alta Via 2 — harder, longer, quieter

The high route for people who have done AV1. More exposed, more via ferrata sections, fewer
people. Not a first Dolomites trek.

### Shorter hut-to-hut loops — 3 to 5 days

The under-used option. Circuits around the Sella massif, the Sassolungo group, or the
Fanes–Sennes plateau give you the hut experience over a long weekend, and they are
substantially easier to book than a full AV1 chain.

**For most people with a normal holiday allowance, this is the better trip.**

### Day hiking from a base — no booking pressure

Stay in Ortisei, Cortina d'Ampezzo, or the Val di Funes and walk a different route each day.
Cable cars do the ascent, which turns serious mountains into achievable afternoons.

The essential day walks:

- **Tre Cime di Lavaredo loop** — the three towers, and the single most recognisable
  landscape in the range. Busy. Go early or late; the light is better anyway.
- **Seceda ridgeline** — the tilted grass slope dropping away beneath jagged spires. Cable
  car from Ortisei, then walk.
- **Lago di Braies** — the lake at the AV1 trailhead. Extremely photographed, access
  restricted in peak season, worth it at 7am and not at 11.
- **Cinque Torri** — five towers, open First World War trenches and tunnels, and a short
  circuit that suits a half day.

## What It Costs

Per person per day.

| Style | Daily cost | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| Rifugio, dormitory, half board | €70–€95 | Bed, dinner, breakfast. Drinks and lunch extra |
| Rifugio, private room, half board | €110–€150 | Same, with a door |
| Valley base, mid-range hotel | €120–€180 | Room, breakfast, cable cars |
| Guided hut-to-hut group tour | €3,000–€5,000 for the week | Guide, huts, most meals, luggage handling, transfers |

**Half board is standard and worth taking.** There is nowhere else to eat, and the food is
better than it has any business being.

**Budget extras:** cable cars run €15–€35 a ride and add up fast. Cash matters — some huts
still don't take cards, and mobile signal is unreliable.

## Getting There and Around

**Airports:** Venice Marco Polo for the south and east, Innsbruck for the north, Verona or
Milan as fallbacks. Innsbruck is closest to the Val Pusteria and Alta Badia.

**Car or no car:** a car is genuinely useful for day hiking from a base — trailheads are
scattered and buses are seasonal. For a hut-to-hut traverse a car is a liability, because
you finish somewhere else entirely.

**Public transport** in South Tyrol is better than most mountain regions in Europe. The
Val Pusteria rail line plus regional buses will get you to most trailheads in summer. Check
the summer timetable, not the annual one — many services only run in season.

**The language thing:** South Tyrol is German-speaking and bilingual. Every place has two
names — Bolzano is Bozen, Ortisei is St. Ulrich, Val Gardena is Gröden. Maps and signs
switch between them. Note both when planning.

## Guided or Independent?

Independent is entirely achievable. The trails are well marked with numbered signposts,
the huts speak English, and the route-finding is not the hard part.

**Where a guided trip earns its cost:** it removes the booking problem completely. An
operator holds hut allocations well in advance, which sidesteps the December scramble
entirely — and if you have decided late, it may be the only way to walk a popular route in
peak season at all. Luggage moves separately, which changes the walking considerably.
Small-group operators including EF Adventures run guided Dolomites hiking departures on
roughly that model.

**Where it doesn't:** if you are organised enough to book in December, independent hut-to-hut
costs a fraction of the guided price for a very similar walk. The huts are the same huts.

## What to Pack

Specific to this range, not a general hiking list.

- **Broken-in boots with ankle support.** Dolomite limestone is sharp, loose and unforgiving
  of trainers.
- **A hut sheet or silk liner.** Required at most rifugios; blankets are provided, sheets
  often are not.
- **Earplugs.** Dormitories. Non-negotiable.
- **Cash in euros.** Card acceptance is patchy above the valley floor.
- **A proper waterproof.** Afternoon thunderstorms are routine in July and August and arrive
  quickly.
- **Layers for near-freezing mornings** even in August at 2,500 m.
- **Indoor shoes.** Boots come off at the door; most huts provide crocs, but not always in
  your size.
- **A power bank.** Charging points are limited and often paid.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**When should I book Dolomites rifugios?**
Between December and February for the following summer. Booking systems open in that window
and the peak July–August dates at popular huts are typically gone by March. Private rooms
need the longest lead time; dormitory beds last a little longer.

**How hard is the Alta Via 1?**
Demanding but not technical. It runs 125 km over 8–12 days, with 5–7 hours of walking and
800–1,000 m of ascent on a typical day. No climbing experience or via ferrata equipment is
required, though some sections have fixed cables. The difficulty is the accumulation, not
any single day.

**When is the best time to hike in the Dolomites?**
Mid-July to mid-September for reliable conditions — trails clear of snow, all huts open.
The wider season runs mid-June to the end of September; June can hold snow on high passes
and late September brings hut closures.

**Can you hike the Dolomites without staying in mountain huts?**
Yes, and for many people it is the better trip. Basing yourself in Ortisei, Cortina
d'Ampezzo or the Val di Funes and day hiking with cable car assistance gives you the same
landscapes with no booking pressure and no multi-day commitment.

---

## The Bottom Line

The Dolomites are the most accessible high-mountain walking in Europe, and the only real
barrier is a booking window that opens in December and closes in March. Fix your dates this
autumn, book the huts before the flights, and the rest of the trip organises itself.
